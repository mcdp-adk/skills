# Creative and Experimental Projects

Read this reference when creative or experimental work depends on experience, taste, prototype learning, or other results that automated checks cannot judge reliably. Apply the same four responsibilities and layers, while preserving the evidence and authority distinctions particular to this work.

## Map creative responsibilities conditionally

Project-specific carriers may commonly relate as follows:

- a Vision or experience goal often carries **goal**;
- a GDD may be a broad container for several responsibilities, or the project may use no GDD;
- mechanics, systems, interaction, or technical design often carries **design**;
- consequential creative choices and their rationale carry **decisions**;
- prototype, production, or learning milestones carry **plans**.

These are responsibility mappings, not required names or a fixed directory. Let the project's existing forms and readers determine the arrangement.

## Relate intent, systems, dynamics, and experience

Creative work may begin with an intended experience: a mood, tension, rhythm, visual direction, sense of agency, or kind of curiosity. Mechanisms and systems produce actual dynamics, which people encounter as experience. MDA (Mechanics, Dynamics, Aesthetics) is a useful lens for understanding this relationship:

```text
mechanics → dynamics → experience
```

Preserve the relationship in the carrier that readers can inspect. Keep intended experience distinct from observed behavior and from a person's interpretation of that behavior. When the relationship is uncertain, connect the hypothesis to the relevant prototype, scene, parameter set, recording, or other artifact.

## Distinguish delivery from learning

A **delivery milestone** produces a known result such as a feature, scene, build, or integration. A **learning milestone** reduces an important uncertainty such as whether a mechanic creates the desired rhythm or whether an interaction is worth keeping. A prototype may succeed by answering its question rather than becoming production-ready; its outcome can be `KEEP`, `MODIFY`, or `KILL` with appropriate reasoning and evidence.

Keep both milestone kinds in the project's existing plan or experiment record. If the result changes a durable goal, design, decision, or plan, update that authoritative knowledge after the work.

## Use faithful carriers and evidence

An interactive or multimodal artifact may communicate experience more faithfully than prose. Depending on the question, connect the relevant prototype, scene, build, video, audio, asset, parameter set, telemetry, playtest observation, or participant response, and state what the reader should inspect.

Distinguish evidence by what it can support:

- **Technical evidence** shows that code, assets, scenes, builds, or integrations behave as claimed.
- **Behavioral evidence** shows what the system does across inputs, states, or observed runs, including measured behavior and structured playtest observations.
- **Human experiential evidence** addresses whether people understand, enjoy, feel, notice, or value the result, and whether it matches the intended direction.

An Agent can analyze recordings, propose criteria, simulate users, or identify reachable-state problems. Treat that as Agent analysis with stated evidence and limits. Real user or human stakeholder feedback is distinct experiential evidence; when taste, aesthetic direction, conceptual risk, or final experience is at issue, identify the authority that can confirm the resulting direction.

## Apply it to the actual project

Before substantial creative or experimental work, read the relevant goal, design, decisions, and plan sources, then inspect the artifact and evidence needed to experience or evaluate the work. During work, keep observations and temporary progress distinct from lasting learning. After work, update only the durable responsibility changed by the evidence. If the project already provides a reliable path from intent to artifact to learning, continue using it.
